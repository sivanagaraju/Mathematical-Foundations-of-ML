# Latent Variable Models: Hidden Structure Discovery and Marginal Inference

[Module guide](README.md) · [Study routes](START_HERE.md) · Previous: [Autoregressive models](04-Autoregressive_Models.md) · Next: [Expectation-maximization algorithm](06-Expectation_Maximization_Algorithm.md)

## 1. What this idea helps you do

Real-world observations (such as photographs of human faces, medical sensor streams, or audio recordings) contain thousands to millions of correlated, high-dimensional features. Yet these complex observations are typically generated by a much smaller number of underlying physical causes—such as 3D facial orientation, illumination, vocal cord frequency, or health status. Directly fitting an unconstrained probability density $p(x)$ over millions of pixels without hidden variables is statistically impossible due to the curse of dimensionality.

A **Latent Variable Model (LVM)** formulates the generative process by introducing unobserved, hidden variables $z \in \mathcal{Z}$. The joint distribution factorizes into a prior over latent causes $p(z)$ and a conditional observation likelihood $p_\theta(x \mid z)$. However, evaluating the marginal likelihood (evidence) of observed data requires integrating over all possible latent causes:

$$p_\theta(x) = \int_{\mathcal{Z}} p_\theta(x \mid z) p(z) \, dz$$

In deep neural networks, this continuous integral is mathematically intractable. This chapter establishes the foundational mathematics of latent variables, derives why exact marginalization fails, proves the **Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO)** identity, and prepares the tools needed for Expectation-Maximization and Variational Autoencoders.

```text
================================================================================
```

*What to notice from the diagram:*
1. The forward generative process ($z \to x$) is straightforward: sample a compact latent vector $z \sim p(z)$ and evaluate the decoder $p_\theta(x \mid z)$.
2. The reverse inference problem ($x \to z$) is intractable because calculating the true posterior $p_\theta(z \mid x)$ requires dividing by the marginal evidence $p(x)$.
3. Evaluating the marginal evidence $p(x)$ requires an intractable integral over the entire continuous latent space $\mathcal{Z}$.

## 3. Name the objects and read the notation

Let $x \in \mathcal{X} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^D$ be an observed data vector, and let $z \in \mathcal{Z} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d$ be an unobserved latent vector, where typically $d \ll D$.

A **Latent Variable Model** is parameterized by $\theta$ and specifies a joint distribution:

$$p_\theta(x, z) \triangleq p_\theta(x \mid z) p(z)$$

1. **Prior Distribution ($p(z)$):** The probability density over latent factors before observing data. Typically a standard isotropic Gaussian:
   $$p(z) \triangleq \mathcal{N}(z \mid 0, I_d) = (2\pi)^{-d/2} \exp\left( -\frac{1}{2} \|z\|_2^2 \right)$$
2. **Conditional Likelihood / Decoder ($p_\theta(x \mid z)$):** The generative mechanism rendering observation $x$ from latent code $z$. In a continuous Gaussian model with neural decoder $g_\theta$:
   $$p_\theta(x \mid z) \triangleq \mathcal{N}(x \mid g_\theta(z), \sigma^2 I_D)$$
3. **Marginal Likelihood / Evidence ($p_\theta(x)$):** The total probability of observing $x$, marginalizing over all possible latent causes:
   $$p_\theta(x) \triangleq \int_{\mathcal{Z}} p_\theta(x, z) \, dz = \int_{\mathcal{Z}} p_\theta(x \mid z) p(z) \, dz$$
4. **True Posterior Distribution ($p_\theta(z \mid x)$):** The conditional probability over latent causes given observation $x$:
   $$p_\theta(z \mid x) \triangleq \frac{p_\theta(x, z)}{p_\theta(x)} = \frac{p_\theta(x \mid z) p(z)}{\int_{\mathcal{Z}} p_\theta(x \mid z') p(z') \, dz'}$$
5. **Variational Distribution ($q_\phi(z \mid x)$):** A tractable proposal distribution (the inference encoder) parameterized by $\phi$ that approximates the intractable true posterior $p_\theta(z \mid x)$.

Read Bayes' posterior rule aloud:  
*“The posterior probability of latent cause z given observation x equals the conditional likelihood of x given z times the prior probability of z, divided by the marginal evidence p of x.”*

| Symbol | Spoken as | Mathematical role / dimensions | Concrete toy value (§2 / §9) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $x$ | “ex” | Observed data vector; $\mathbb{R}^D$ | $x = 7.5000 \in \mathbb{R}^1$ ($D=1$) |
| $z$ | “zee” | Unobserved latent variable; $\mathbb{R}^d$ or $\{1..K\}$ | $z \in \{1, 2\}$ (discrete cluster) |
| $p(z)$ | “p of zee” | Latent prior distribution | Mixture weights: $\pi_1 = 0.3, \pi_2 = 0.7$ |

| $p_\theta(x)$ | “p sub theta of ex” | Marginal likelihood (Evidence) | $p(x = 7.5) \approx 0.246446$ |

## 4. Build the central relationship

### The Core "Aha!" Discovery

Visible data $x$ is just the 2D shadow cast on a wall; the latent variable $z$ is the 3D wooden puppet behind the curtain. If we can infer the hidden puppet coordinates, we can manipulate, interpolate, and generate brand new photorealistic observations by sampling fresh latent codes $z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$!

### Step-by-Step Proof: Analytical Marginalization in Linear-Gaussian Models

To understand why deep latent models require approximations, let us first prove how linear-Gaussian models achieve exact closed-form marginalization via the **completion-of-the-square** method.

#### Setting and Definitions
Let $z \in \mathbb{R}^d$ and $x \in \mathbb{R}^D$. Consider the linear generative process:
- Prior: $p(z) = \mathcal{N}(z \mid 0, I_d) = (2\pi)^{-d/2} \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2} z^\top z\right)$
- Likelihood: $p(x \mid z) = \mathcal{N}(x \mid W z, \sigma^2 I_D) = (2\pi \sigma^2)^{-D/2} \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2} \|x - W z\|_2^2\right)$
where $W \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times d}$ is the emission matrix and $\sigma^2 > 0$ is the isotropic observation noise variance.

We wish to evaluate the marginal evidence:

$$p(x) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} p(x \mid z) p(z) \, dz$$

#### Step 1: Formulate the Joint Exponent
The joint density $p(x, z) = p(x \mid z) p(z)$ has exponent $-\frac{1}{2} Q(x, z)$, where:

$$Q(x, z) = z^\top z + \frac{1}{\sigma^2} (x - W z)^\top (x - W z)$$

Expanding the squared Euclidean norm:

$$Q(x, z) = z^\top z + \frac{1}{\sigma^2} \left( x^\top x - 2 x^\top W z + z^\top W^\top W z \right)$$

Collecting quadratic, linear, and constant terms with respect to $z$:

$$Q(x, z) = z^\top \underbrace{\left( I_d + \frac{1}{\sigma^2} W^\top W \right)}_{A} z - 2 \underbrace{\left( \frac{1}{\sigma^2} W^\top x \right)^\top}_{b^\top} z + \frac{1}{\sigma^2} x^\top x$$

Here, $A \triangleq I_d + \frac{1}{\sigma^2} W^\top W \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is symmetric strictly positive definite, and $b \triangleq \frac{1}{\sigma^2} W^\top x \in \mathbb{R}^d$.

#### Step 2: Complete the Square in $z$
Rewriting the quadratic form in $z$:

$$z^\top A z - 2 b^\top z = (z - A^{-1} b)^\top A (z - A^{-1} b) - b^\top A^{-1} b$$

Substituting this back into $Q(x, z)$:

$$Q(x, z) = (z - A^{-1} b)^\top A (z - A^{-1} b) + \left( \frac{1}{\sigma^2} x^\top x - b^\top A^{-1} b \right)$$

Notice that the second term is completely independent of $z$!

#### Step 3: Integrate over $z$
Substituting $Q(x, z)$ into the marginal integral:

$$p(x) = (2\pi)^{-(D+d)/2} (\sigma^2)^{-D/2} \exp\left( -\frac{1}{2} \left[ \frac{1}{\sigma^2} x^\top x - b^\top A^{-1} b \right] \right) \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \exp\left( -\frac{1}{2} (z - A^{-1} b)^\top A (z - A^{-1} b) \right) dz$$

The integral is a standard unnormalized Gaussian integral over $\mathbb{R}^d$, which evaluates to $(2\pi)^{d/2} |A|^{-1/2}$:

$$p(x) = (2\pi)^{-D/2} (\sigma^2)^{-D/2} |A|^{-1/2} \exp\left( -\frac{1}{2} \left[ \frac{1}{\sigma^2} x^\top x - b^\top A^{-1} b \right] \right)$$

#### Step 4: Simplify via Matrix Determinant and Woodbury Inversion
Substituting $b = \frac{1}{\sigma^2} W^\top x$ and $A = I_d + \frac{1}{\sigma^2} W^\top W$:

1. **Exponent Term:**
   $$\frac{1}{\sigma^2} x^\top x - b^\top A^{-1} b = x^\top \left( \frac{1}{\sigma^2} I_D - \frac{1}{\sigma^4} W \left( I_d + \frac{1}{\sigma^2} W^\top W \right)^{-1} W^\top \right) x$$
   By the Woodbury matrix inversion lemma $(A + U C V)^{-1} = A^{-1} - A^{-1} U (C^{-1} + V A^{-1} U)^{-1} V A^{-1}$:
   $$\left( W W^\top + \sigma^2 I_D \right)^{-1} = \frac{1}{\sigma^2} I_D - \frac{1}{\sigma^4} W \left( I_d + \frac{1}{\sigma^2} W^\top W \right)^{-1} W^\top$$
2. **Determinant Term:**
   By Sylvester's determinant theorem $|I_d + \frac{1}{\sigma^2} W^\top W| = |I_D + \frac{1}{\sigma^2} W W^\top|$:
   $$(\sigma^2)^{-D/2} |A|^{-1/2} = \left| \sigma^2 I_D + W W^\top \right|^{-1/2}$$

Combining these identities yields the exact closed-form marginal distribution:

$$\boxed{p(x) = \mathcal{N}\left( x \mid 0, \; W W^\top + \sigma^2 I_D \right)}$$

And the true posterior distribution is also Gaussian:

$$\boxed{p(z \mid x) = \mathcal{N}\left( z \mid \mu_{z|x}, \Sigma_{z|x} \right), \quad \Sigma_{z|x} = \left( I_d + \frac{1}{\sigma^2} W^\top W \right)^{-1}, \quad \mu_{z|x} = \Sigma_{z|x} \frac{1}{\sigma^2} W^\top x}$$

#### Why This Breaks Down in Deep Latent Models
In a Variational Autoencoder, the linear emission $W z$ is replaced by a deep non-linear neural network $g_\theta(z)$. The exponent becomes:

$$Q(x, z) = z^\top z + \frac{1}{\sigma^2} \|x - g_\theta(z)\|_2^2$$

Because $g_\theta(z)$ contains non-linear activation functions (ReLU, GELU, Swish), $Q(x, z)$ is no longer quadratic in $z$. We cannot complete the square, and the integral cannot be solved analytically.

### Step-by-Step Proof: The Master ELBO Identity

Since deep marginal integrals are intractable, we derive the exact algebraic identity that allows us to optimize a tractable lower bound:

1. Let $q_\phi(z \mid x)$ be any valid probability density on $\mathcal{Z}$ such that $q_\phi(z \mid x) > 0$ whenever $p_\theta(x, z) > 0$.  
   Since $\ln p_\theta(x)$ is constant with respect to $z$ and $\int q_\phi(z \mid x) dz = 1$:
   $$\ln p_\theta(x) = \int q_\phi(z \mid x) \ln p_\theta(x) \, dz$$

2. By Bayes' rule, $p_\theta(x) = \frac{p_\theta(x, z)}{p_\theta(z \mid x)}$. Substitute into the logarithm:
   $$\ln p_\theta(x) = \int q_\phi(z \mid x) \ln\left( \frac{p_\theta(x, z)}{p_\theta(z \mid x)} \right) dz$$

3. Multiply and divide inside the logarithm by the variational proposal $q_\phi(z \mid x)$:
   $$\ln p_\theta(x) = \int q_\phi(z \mid x) \ln\left( \frac{p_\theta(x, z)}{q_\phi(z \mid x)} \cdot \frac{q_\phi(z \mid x)}{p_\theta(z \mid x)} \right) dz$$

4. Apply the logarithmic product rule $\ln(A \cdot B) = \ln A + \ln B$:
   $$\ln p_\theta(x) = \int q_\phi(z \mid x) \ln\left( \frac{p_\theta(x, z)}{q_\phi(z \mid x)} \right) dz + \int q_\phi(z \mid x) \ln\left( \frac{q_\phi(z \mid x)}{p_\theta(z \mid x)} \right) dz$$

5. Recognize the two terms:
   - The first term is the **Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO)**:
     $$\text{ELBO}(\phi, \theta; x) \triangleq \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z \mid x)}\left[ \ln\left( \frac{p_\theta(x, z)}{q_\phi(z \mid x)} \right) \right]$$
   - The second term is the **Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence** from the true posterior to $q$:
     $$D_{\text{KL}}\left( q_\phi(z \mid x) \parallel p_\theta(z \mid x) \right) \triangleq \int q_\phi(z \mid x) \ln\left( \frac{q_\phi(z \mid x)}{p_\theta(z \mid x)} \right) dz$$

6. Combining both yields the Master Decomposition:
   $$\boxed{\ln p_\theta(x) = \text{ELBO}(\phi, \theta; x) + D_{\text{KL}}\left( q_\phi(z \mid x) \parallel p_\theta(z \mid x) \right)}$$

7. By Gibbs' inequality, $D_{\text{KL}}(P \parallel Q) \ge 0$, with equality if and only if $q_\phi(z \mid x) = p_\theta(z \mid x)$ almost everywhere. Therefore:
   $$\boxed{\text{ELBO}(\phi, \theta; x) \le \ln p_\theta(x)}$$

```text
================================================================================
                    THE LATENT VARIABLE INFERENCE TRIANGLE
================================================================================
                                  ln p_theta(x)
                             (Marginal Log-Evidence)
                                       ▲
                                      ╱ ╲
                ELBO(phi, theta; x) ╱   ╲   D_KL( q_phi(z|x) || p_theta(z|x) )
              (Tractable Bound)    ╱     ╲ (Intractable Variational Gap)
                                  ╱       ╲
                                 ▼─────────▼
                              q_phi(z|x)  p_theta(z|x)
================================================================================
```

*What to notice from the diagram:*
1. The true marginal log-evidence $\ln p_\theta(x)$ is the sum of two components: the tractable ELBO and the non-negative KL divergence gap.
2. When the variational encoder $q_\phi(z \mid x)$ matches the true posterior $p_\theta(z \mid x)$ perfectly, the KL gap shrinks to 0, and the ELBO is exactly equal to the true log-evidence.

### Proof of Fisher's Identity for Marginal Log-Likelihood Gradients

How do we compute the gradient of the marginal log-likelihood $\nabla_\theta \ln p_\theta(x)$ when latents are present?

#### Theorem 4.2: Fisher's Identity
If $p_\theta(x, z)$ is differentiable with respect to $\theta$ and there exists an integrable function $g(z)$ such that $\|\nabla_\theta p_\theta(x, z)\| \le g(z)$ for all $\theta$ in a neighborhood of $\theta_0$ (the Dominated Convergence condition), then:

$$\nabla_\theta \ln p_\theta(x) = \mathbb{E}_{p_\theta(z \mid x)}\left[ \nabla_\theta \ln p_\theta(x, z) \right]$$

#### Proof:
1. By definition of the logarithm derivative:
   $$\nabla_\theta \ln p_\theta(x) = \frac{\nabla_\theta p_\theta(x)}{p_\theta(x)} = \frac{\nabla_\theta \int_{\mathcal{Z}} p_\theta(x, z) \, dz}{p_\theta(x)}$$
2. Under the dominated convergence condition, the Leibniz integral rule permits exchanging the gradient operator and the integral:
   $$\nabla_\theta \int_{\mathcal{Z}} p_\theta(x, z) \, dz = \int_{\mathcal{Z}} \nabla_\theta p_\theta(x, z) \, dz$$
3. Use the logarithmic identity $\nabla_\theta p_\theta(x, z) = p_\theta(x, z) \nabla_\theta \ln p_\theta(x, z)$:
   $$= \int_{\mathcal{Z}} p_\theta(x, z) \nabla_\theta \ln p_\theta(x, z) \, dz$$
4. Dividing by the scalar $p_\theta(x)$:
   $$\nabla_\theta \ln p_\theta(x) = \int_{\mathcal{Z}} \frac{p_\theta(x, z)}{p_\theta(x)} \nabla_\theta \ln p_\theta(x, z) \, dz$$
5. By Bayes' rule, $\frac{p_\theta(x, z)}{p_\theta(x)} = p_\theta(z \mid x)$. Therefore:
   $$\boxed{\nabla_\theta \ln p_\theta(x) = \int_{\mathcal{Z}} p_\theta(z \mid x) \nabla_\theta \ln p_\theta(x, z) \, dz = \mathbb{E}_{p_\theta(z \mid x)}\left[ \nabla_\theta \ln p_\theta(x, z) \right]}$$
This proves that the gradient of the marginal log-likelihood is the posterior expectation of the complete-data log-likelihood gradient! $\blacksquare$

### Concrete Mathematical Failure Counterexample: Curse of Dimensionality in Naive Monte Carlo Marginalization

Suppose a model has a 32-dimensional continuous latent space $z \in \mathbb{R}^{32}$ with standard Gaussian prior $p(z) = \mathcal{N}(0, I_{32})$. We wish to estimate the marginal likelihood of an observed image $x$ using naive Monte Carlo sampling from the prior:

$$p(x) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^{32}} p_\theta(x \mid z) p(z) \, dz \approx \frac{1}{S} \sum_{s=1}^S p_\theta(x \mid z^{(s)}), \qquad z^{(s)} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_{32})$$

1. **Volume of the Target Ball:** In 32 dimensions, the volume of a sphere of radius $r$ scales as $r^{32}$. If the decoder only renders a plausible reconstruction when $z$ is within radius $r = 0.1$ of the true code, the fraction of prior volume occupied by valid codes is:
   $$\frac{\text{Vol}(B_{0.1})}{\text{Vol}(B_{1.0})} = \left(\frac{0.1}{1.0}\right)^{32} = 10^{-32}$$
2. **Probability of Zero Hits:** If you draw $S = 100,000$ random samples from the prior $p(z)$, the probability that **every single sample** misses the target region is:
   $$\left(1 - 10^{-32}\right)^{100,000} \approx 1 - 10^{-27}$$
3. **Catastrophic Estimation Variance:** Every sampled point evaluates to $p_\theta(x \mid z^{(s)}) \approx 0$. The sample average evaluates to 0, yielding $\ln p(x) \approx -\infty$.
4. **Why Variational Inference is Mandatory:** Naive prior sampling searches blindly in high-dimensional space. An inference network $q_\phi(z \mid x)$ inspects the image $x$ directly and samples specifically in the tiny region where $p(x \mid z) > 0$.

We have established why naive prior integration fails. Next, we examine real-world mental models and their breaking points.

## 7. Terms worth keeping straight

### Core Terminology Reference Table

| Term | Pronunciation | Plain-English Meaning | Formal Definition & Conditions |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Latent Variable ($z$)** | “LAY-tunt VAIR-ee-uh-bul” | Unobserved hidden variable generating visible patterns | Unmeasured cause $z \in \mathcal{Z}$ governing observed data $x \in \mathcal{X}$. |
| **Observed Variable ($x$)** | “ub-ZERVD VAIR-ee-uh-bul” | Empirical measurements directly available in dataset | Measurable data points $x \sim p_{\text{data}}(x)$ (pixels, audio, text). |
| **Marginal Likelihood** | “MAR-juh-nul LYK-lee-hood” | Total probability of data integrating over all possible hidden causes | $p(x) = \int p(x, z) dz$. Often an intractable high-dimensional integral. |
| **Complete Likelihood** | “kum-PLEET LYK-lee-hood” | Joint probability of observing both data and latent variable | $p(x, z) = p(x \mid z) p(z)$. Computationally tractable joint density. |
| **Posterior ($p(z \mid x)$)** | “pos-TEER-ee-er” | Probability distribution over hidden causes given an observed data point | $p(z \mid x) = \frac{p(x \mid z) p(z)}{p(x)}$. Usually intractable without approximations. |
| **Amortized Inference** | “AM-or-tyzd IN-fer-uns” | Predicting variational parameters for any input using a single shared network | $q_\phi(z \mid x)$ maps $x \mapsto (\mu_\phi(x), \sigma_\phi(x))$ in one forward pass. |
| **Posterior Collapse** | “pos-TEER-ee-er kuh-LAPS” | Failure mode where latent variables are ignored by decoder | $q_\phi(z \mid x) \approx p(z)$ and $p_\theta(x \mid z) \approx p(x)$; $z$ carries zero information. |
| **Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO)**| “EL-boh” | Tractable variational lower bound on intractable log-marginal likelihood | $\log p(x) \ge \mathbb{E}_q[\log p(x \mid z)] - D_{\text{KL}}(q(z \mid x) \parallel p(z))$. |

### Confused Pairs Distinction Breakdown

1. **Latent Variable ($z$) vs. Observed Variable ($x$)**:
   - *Core Distinction:* Observed variables are empirically measured; latent variables are unmeasured theoretical constructs.
   - *Common Confusion:* Conflating latent variables in probabilistic models with hidden layer activations in deterministic neural networks.
   - *Rule of Thumb:* Latent variables have explicit probability distributions ($p(z), q(z \mid x)$); hidden activations are deterministic tensor values.
- **Observed Variable ($x$):** Directly measured empirical data (pixel matrices, text tokens, acoustic waveforms).
- **Latent Variable ($z$):** Hidden, unobserved random variable postulated by the model to explain correlations in $x$.

### 2. Prior $p(z)$ vs. Likelihood $p(x \mid z)$ vs. Posterior $p(z \mid x)$
- **Prior $p(z)$:** Belief about latent distribution before observing data ($z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$).
- **Likelihood $p(x \mid z)$:** Generative model rendering observations from a given latent code.
- **Posterior $p(z \mid x)$:** Distribution over latent codes after observing data point $x$.

### 3. Marginal Evidence $p(x)$ vs. Complete Joint Likelihood $p(x, z)$
- **Complete Joint Likelihood $p(x, z)$:** Probability of observing both data $x$ and latent $z$ together. Tractable to evaluate.
- **Marginal Evidence $p(x) = \int p(x, z) dz$:** Probability of observing data $x$ alone, integrating over all possible $z$. Intractable in deep continuous networks.

### 4. Amortized Inference vs. Per-Sample Latent Optimization
- **Per-Sample Optimization (EM / MAP):** Solves an individual optimization problem for every data point $x_i$ to find its optimal latent state $z_i$.
- **Amortized Inference (VAEs):** Trains a single neural network (encoder $q_\phi(z \mid x)$) to predict variational parameters for any input in a single forward pass.

### 5. Posterior Collapse
- **Posterior Collapse:** A training failure where the encoder learns to output the prior $q_\phi(z \mid x) \approx p(z)$ and the decoder ignores $z$ entirely, treating the model as an unconditioned density estimator.

We have clarified key terminology. Next, we work through formal loss formulations, gradient derivations, and hardware realities.

## 8. Work through the mathematics and its conditions

### Optimization Objectives

#### 1. Discrete Mixture Model Evidence
In a mixture model with $K$ discrete components:

$$p(x) = \sum_{k=1}^K \pi_k p(x \mid z = k), \qquad \sum_{k=1}^K \pi_k = 1$$

The posterior responsibility that component $k$ generated observation $x$ is:

$$\gamma_k \triangleq p(z = k \mid x) = \frac{\pi_k p(x \mid z = k)}{\sum_{j=1}^K \pi_j p(x \mid z = j)}$$

#### 2. The Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO) Decomposition
For any variational proposal distribution $q_\phi(z \mid x)$:

$$\text{ELBO}(\phi, \theta) \triangleq \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z \mid x)}\left[ \ln p_\theta(x \mid z) \right] - D_{\text{KL}}\left( q_\phi(z \mid x) \parallel p(z) \right)$$

1. **Reconstruction Term:** $\mathbb{E}_{q_\phi}[\ln p_\theta(x \mid z)]$ rewards the model for reconstructing $x$ accurately from sampled latents.
2. **KL Regularizer:** $-D_{\text{KL}}(q_\phi(z \mid x) \parallel p(z))$ penalizes the encoder for deviating from the simple prior $p(z)$, preventing latent voids.

#### 3. Analytical Fisher's Identity for GMM Cluster Means
Applying Theorem 4.2 to a Gaussian Mixture Model with component distributions $\mathcal{N}(x \mid \mu_k, \sigma_k^2)$:

$$\nabla_{\mu_k} \ln p(x) = \frac{\partial \ln p(x)}{\partial \mu_k} = \gamma_k \cdot \frac{x - \mu_k}{\sigma_k^2}$$

*Sign Interpretation:* The gradient pulls centroid $\mu_k$ toward $x$ in proportion to the component's responsibility $\gamma_k$. If point $x$ has zero responsibility under cluster $k$ ($\gamma_k \approx 0$), it exerts zero pull on $\mu_k$.

### Computational Scaling and Hardware Realities

1. **Quadrature Grid Explosion:** To evaluate $p(x) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} p(x \mid z) p(z) dz$ numerically using a simple 1D Simpson's rule grid with 100 points per dimension:
   - For $d = 1$: requires $100^1 = 100$ evaluations.
   - For $d = 10$: requires $100^{10} = 10^{20}$ evaluations ($3.17\text{ billion years}$ at 1 GHz!).
   - For $d = 64$ (Latent Diffusion): requires $100^{64} = 10^{128}$ evaluations. Deterministic numerical integration is physically impossible.
2. **Monte Carlo Variance Explosion:** As shown in Section 5, drawing samples from prior $p(z)$ yields an estimator $\hat{p}(x) = \frac{1}{S}\sum p(x \mid z^{(s)})$ whose variance-to-mean ratio scales exponentially with dimension $d$, proving why importance-weighted or variational inference is mandatory.

We now have the formal loss formulations, gradient mechanics, and hardware scaling laws. Next, we work through concrete numerical calculations by hand.

## 9. Calculate it by hand

### Worked Example 1: 2-Component Gaussian Mixture Model Forward and Backward Pass

Suppose a 1D dataset is modeled by 2 latent clusters:
- Prior mixture weights: $\pi_1 = 0.30$ (Cluster 1), $\pi_2 = 0.70$ (Cluster 2).
- Cluster 1 parameters: $\mu_1 = 2.0000, \quad \sigma_1 = 1.0000$.
- Cluster 2 parameters: $\mu_2 = 8.0000, \quad \sigma_2 = 1.0000$.
- Observed data point: $x = 7.5000$.
- Learning rate: $\eta = 0.20$.

---

#### Step 1: Compute Component Likelihoods
Recall the 1D Gaussian PDF formula $\mathcal{N}(x \mid \mu, \sigma) = \frac{1}{\sigma \sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\left( -\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2} \right)$, where $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \approx 0.398942$.

1. **For Cluster 1 ($\mu_1 = 2.0, \sigma_1 = 1.0$):**
   $$(x - \mu_1)^2 = (7.50 - 2.00)^2 = (5.50)^2 = 30.25$$
   $$\mathcal{N}_1(7.50) = 0.398942 \times \exp(-0.5 \times 30.25) = 0.398942 \times e^{-15.125} \approx \mathbf{1.0883 \times 10^{-7}}$$
2. **For Cluster 2 ($\mu_2 = 8.0, \sigma_2 = 1.0$):**
   $$(x - \mu_2)^2 = (7.50 - 8.00)^2 = (-0.50)^2 = 0.25$$
   $$\mathcal{N}_2(7.50) = 0.398942 \times \exp(-0.5 \times 0.25) = 0.398942 \times e^{-0.125} = 0.398942 \times 0.882497 \approx \mathbf{0.352065}$$

---

#### Step 2: Compute Marginal Evidence $p(x)$ and Log-Evidence
$$p(x = 7.50) = \pi_1 \mathcal{N}_1(7.50) + \pi_2 \mathcal{N}_2(7.50)$$
$$p(x = 7.50) = (0.30)(1.0883 \times 10^{-7}) + (0.70)(0.352065) = 0.00000003 + 0.2464455 \approx \mathbf{0.246446}$$
$$\ln p(x = 7.50) = \ln(0.246446) \approx \mathbf{-1.400613\text{ nats}}$$

---

#### Step 3: Compute Posterior Responsibilities ($\gamma_k = p(z=k \mid x=7.50)$)
$$\gamma_2 = \frac{\pi_2 \mathcal{N}_2(7.50)}{p(x = 7.50)} = \frac{0.70 \times 0.352065}{0.246446} = \frac{0.246446}{0.246446} \approx \mathbf{0.999999 \quad (99.9999\% \text{ from Cluster 2!})}$$
$$\gamma_1 = 1 - \gamma_2 = \frac{0.30 \times (1.0883 \times 10^{-7})}{0.246446} \approx \mathbf{1.325 \times 10^{-7}}$$

---

#### Step 4: Analytical Gradient of Marginal Log-Likelihood with Respect to Cluster Means
Using Fisher's Identity:

$$\frac{\partial \ln p(x)}{\partial \mu_k} = \gamma_k \cdot \frac{x - \mu_k}{\sigma_k^2}$$

1. **For Cluster 2 ($\mu_2$):**
   $$\frac{\partial \ln p(x)}{\partial \mu_2} = (1.000000) \cdot \left( \frac{7.5000 - 8.0000}{1.0^2} \right) = (1.000000) \cdot (-0.5000) = \mathbf{-0.5000}$$
2. **For Cluster 1 ($\mu_1$):**
   $$\frac{\partial \ln p(x)}{\partial \mu_1} = (1.325 \times 10^{-7}) \cdot \left( \frac{7.5000 - 2.0000}{1.0^2} \right) = (1.325 \times 10^{-7}) \cdot (5.50) \approx \mathbf{+7.288 \times 10^{-7}} \approx \mathbf{0.0000}$$

---

#### Step 5: Gradient Descent Parameter Update
Defining the loss as Negative Log-Likelihood: $\mathcal{L} = -\ln p(x)$:
$$\nabla_{\mu_2} \mathcal{L} = -\frac{\partial \ln p(x)}{\partial \mu_2} = -(-0.5000) = \mathbf{+0.5000}$$

Updating centroid $\mu_2$ with learning rate $\eta = 0.20$:
$$\mu_2^{(1)} = \mu_2^{(0)} - \eta \nabla_{\mu_2} \mathcal{L} = 8.0000 - (0.20)(+0.5000) = 8.0000 - 0.1000 = \mathbf{7.9000}$$

**Physical Sign Interpretation:**
- Because observation $x = 7.50$ lies to the left of centroid $\mu_2 = 8.00$, moving $\mu_2$ downward toward $7.50$ increases data likelihood. The loss gradient is positive ($+0.5000$). Gradient descent subtracts a positive quantity, shifting $\mu_2$ from $8.00$ to $7.90$.
- Cluster 1 receives a gradient of $\approx 0$ because its responsibility is virtually zero ($\gamma_1 \approx 10^{-7}$); data point $7.50$ exerts zero pull on cluster 1.

---

### Worked Example 2: Discrete ELBO Lower Bound Gap Verification

Using exact log-evidence $\ln p(x = 7.50) = \ln(0.246446) = \mathbf{-1.400613\text{ nats}}$:

#### 1. Evaluating Sub-Optimal Proposal $q(z) = [q(z=1) = 0.05, \quad q(z=2) = 0.95]$:
- Joint probability values:
  $$p(x, z=1) = 0.30 \times (1.0883 \times 10^{-7}) = 3.2649 \times 10^{-8}$$
  $$p(x, z=2) = 0.70 \times 0.352065 = 0.246446$$
- Evidence Lower Bound:
  $$\text{ELBO} = \sum_{k=1}^2 q(z=k) \ln\left( \frac{p(x, z=k)}{q(z=k)} \right)$$
  - Component 1: $0.05 \ln\left(\frac{3.2649 \times 10^{-8}}{0.05}\right) = 0.05 \ln(6.5298 \times 10^{-7}) = 0.05 \times (-14.2415) \approx -0.712075\text{ nats}$
  - Component 2: $0.95 \ln\left(\frac{0.246446}{0.95}\right) = 0.95 \ln(0.259417) = 0.95 \times (-1.349314) \approx -1.281848\text{ nats}$
  - Total $\text{ELBO} = -0.712075 - 1.281848 = \mathbf{-1.993923\text{ nats}}$
- **Lower Bound Gap Check:**
  $$\ln p(x) - \text{ELBO} = -1.400613 - (-1.993923) = \mathbf{+0.593310\text{ nats}} \ge 0$$
  This matches the exact Kullback-Leibler divergence $D_{\text{KL}}(q \parallel p(z \mid x)) = +0.593310 \ge 0$!

#### 2. Evaluating Optimal Variational Proposal $q^*(z) = p(z \mid x) = [0.0, 1.0]$:
When the variational distribution equals the true posterior ($q(z=2) = 1.0$):
$$\text{ELBO} = 1.0 \times \ln\left(\frac{0.246446}{1.0}\right) = \ln(0.246446) = \mathbf{-1.400613\text{ nats}}$$
The KL gap shrinks to exactly $0.000000$, and the ELBO equals the exact log-evidence!

## 11. Verify the idea with a small experiment

```python
"""
Latent Variable Models (LVM) Dual-Stage Verification Suite
==========================================================
Part A: Pure Python standard library (built-in math only, zero dependencies).
Part B: PyTorch industrial verification suite with autograd checks,
        finite differences, and high-dimensional Monte Carlo integration analysis.

import math

print("=" * 80)
print("PART A: PURE PYTHON STDLIB GMM INFERENCE & ANALYTICAL GRADIENT SUITE")
print("=" * 80)

# ─── 1. Pure Python 1D Gaussian Probability Density Function ───
def pure_python_gaussian_pdf(x_val, mu_val, sigma_val):
    inv_sigma_sqrt2pi = 1.0 / (sigma_val * math.sqrt(2.0 * math.pi))
    exponent = -0.5 * (((x_val - mu_val) / sigma_val) ** 2)
    return inv_sigma_sqrt2pi * math.exp(exponent)

# Model configuration from Section 9 Example 1
pi_1, pi_2 = 0.30, 0.70
mu_1, mu_2 = 2.0000, 8.0000
sigma_1, sigma_2 = 1.0000, 1.0000
x = 7.5000

# 1. Component Likelihoods
p_x_z1 = pure_python_gaussian_pdf(x, mu_1, sigma_1)
p_x_z2 = pure_python_gaussian_pdf(x, mu_2, sigma_2)

# 2. Marginal Evidence p(x)
marginal_evidence = pi_1 * p_x_z1 + pi_2 * p_x_z2
log_evidence = math.log(marginal_evidence)

# 3. Posterior Responsibilities
gamma_1 = (pi_1 * p_x_z1) / marginal_evidence
gamma_2 = (pi_2 * p_x_z2) / marginal_evidence

print(f"1. Pure Python Forward Inference (Observed x = {x}):")
print(f"   * Cluster 1 Likelihood:        {p_x_z1:.4e} (Expected: ~1.088e-7)")
print(f"   * Cluster 2 Likelihood:        {p_x_z2:.6f} (Expected: ~0.352065)")
print(f"   * Marginal Evidence p(x):      {marginal_evidence:.6f} (Expected: 0.246446)")
print(f"   * Log-Evidence ln p(x):        {log_evidence:.6f} nats (Expected: -1.400613)")
print(f"   * Responsibility gamma_2:      {gamma_2:.6f} (Expected: ~1.000000)")

assert math.isclose(marginal_evidence, 0.246446, rel_tol=1e-4)
assert math.isclose(gamma_2, 1.000000, rel_tol=1e-5)
assert math.isclose(log_evidence, -1.400613, rel_tol=1e-4)

# 4. Analytical Gradient of Negative Log-Likelihood Loss w.r.t mu_2
# L = -ln p(x) ==> dL/dmu_2 = - gamma_2 * (x - mu_2) / sigma_2^2
grad_loss_mu2 = -gamma_2 * (x - mu_2) / (sigma_2 ** 2)
grad_loss_mu1 = -gamma_1 * (x - mu_1) / (sigma_1 ** 2)

print("\n2. Analytical Gradients of NLL Loss (L = -ln p(x)):")
print(f"   * grad_loss_mu2:               {grad_loss_mu2:.6f} (Expected: +0.500000)")
print(f"   * grad_loss_mu1:               {grad_loss_mu1:.4e} (Expected: ~0.000000)")

assert abs(grad_loss_mu1) < 1e-5

mu_2_updated = mu_2 - 0.20 * grad_loss_mu2
print("\n3. Gradient Descent Step:")
print(f"   * Updated mu_2:                {mu_2_updated:.4f} (Expected: 7.9000)")
assert math.isclose(mu_2_updated, 7.9000, rel_tol=1e-4)

q_z = [0.05, 0.95]
joint_p1 = pi_1 * p_x_z1
joint_p2 = pi_2 * p_x_z2

elbo_val = q_z[0] * math.log(joint_p1 / q_z[0]) + q_z[1] * math.log(joint_p2 / q_z[1])
kl_gap = log_evidence - elbo_val

print("\n4. ELBO Lower Bound Gap:")
print(f"   * ELBO:                        {elbo_val:.6f} nats (Expected: -1.993923)")
print(f"   * KL Divergence Gap:           {kl_gap:.6f} nats (Strictly >= 0.0! [OK])")
print(f"   * Lower Bound Inequality:      {log_evidence >= elbo_val} [OK]")

assert log_evidence >= elbo_val, "ELBO must be <= true log evidence!"
print("Part A Pure Python Suite: ALL CHECKS PASSED [OK]")

print("\n" + "=" * 80)
print("PART B: PYTORCH INDUSTRIAL VERIFICATION & HIGH-DIMENSIONAL MC SUITE")
print("=" * 80)
```

1. **Grid Quadrature Explosion:**

   - Marginal evidence: $p(x) = 0.5(0.20) + 0.5(0.60) = 0.10 + 0.30 = \mathbf{0.4000}$.  
   - Posterior responsibility $\gamma_2$:  
     $$\gamma_2 = \frac{0.5 \times 0.60}{0.40} = \frac{0.30}{0.40} = \mathbf{0.7500 \quad (75\%)}$$  
   - Gradient via Fisher's Identity:  
     $$\frac{\partial \ln p(x)}{\partial \mu_2} = \gamma_2 \cdot \frac{x - \mu_2}{\sigma_2^2} = 0.7500 \cdot \left(\frac{4.0 - 3.0}{1.0}\right) = 0.7500 \times 1.0 = \mathbf{+0.7500}$$  
   *Diagnostic feedback:* If you omitted $\gamma_2$, you forgot that observations pull cluster centroids in direct proportion to their posterior responsibility.

3. **Contrast (Linear-Gaussian vs. Deep VAE):**  
   - In a linear-Gaussian model, linear operations preserve Gaussianity. The exponent remains strictly quadratic in $z$, allowing exact analytical completion of the square.  
   - In a deep VAE, the decoder $g_\theta(z)$ applies non-linear activations (GELU, Swish). The exponent $\|x - g_\theta(z)\|^2$ is non-convex and non-quadratic, making the integral non-elementary and forcing approximate variational inference.

4. **Transfer (ELBO Identity Mechanics):**  
   - By the Master ELBO identity:  
     $$\ln p(x) = \text{ELBO} + D_{\text{KL}}(q \parallel p) = -5.2 + 0.8 = \mathbf{-4.4\text{ nats}}$$  
   - When $\theta$ is fixed, the true marginal likelihood $\ln p(x)$ is **completely constant** (it remains $-4.4\text{ nats}$).  
   - As $D_{\text{KL}}$ decreases from $0.8$ to $0.2$, the ELBO must increase to compensate:  
     $$\text{ELBO} = \ln p(x) - D_{\text{KL}} = -4.4 - 0.2 = \mathbf{-4.6\text{ nats}}$$  
     The ELBO tightens toward the true marginal likelihood!

5. **Debug (Posterior Collapse):**  
   - **Failure Mode:** **Posterior Collapse**. When paired with a strong autoregressive decoder, the model finds that ignoring $z$ and setting $q_\phi(z \mid x) \approx p(z)$ instantly zeroes out the KL divergence penalty without incurring heavy reconstruction penalties early in training. The latent code becomes uninformative static.  
   - **Remedies:**  
     1. *KL Annealing / Warmup:* Multiply the KL divergence by a weighting factor $\beta$, ramping $\beta$ gradually from $0.0 \to 1.0$ over early training epochs so the decoder learns to use $z$ first.  
     2. *Free Bits / KL Thresholding:* Enforce a minimum KL cost per latent dimension: $\max(\tau, D_{\text{KL}})$, ensuring the optimizer never benefits from collapsing $z$ below threshold $\tau$.  
     3. *Weaken the Decoder:* Use a decoder with a limited receptive field so it is forced to rely on the global latent code $z$.

### Transfer Challenge: Bayesian Linear Regression Posterior Derivation

**Scenario:** A 1-dimensional continuous latent model has prior $p(z) = \mathcal{N}(z \mid 0, 1)$ and conditional observation likelihood $p(x \mid z) = \mathcal{N}(x \mid w z, \sigma^2)$, where weight $w = 2.0$ and observation noise variance $\sigma^2 = 1.0$.

1. **Calculate the Exact Marginal Distribution $p(x)$:** Compute the exact marginal mean $\mathbb{E}[x]$ and variance $\text{Var}(x) = w^2 \text{Var}(z) + \sigma^2$. State $p(x)$.
2. **Derive the Posterior Distribution $p(z \mid x)$:** Compute the posterior mean $\mu_{z|x}$ and posterior variance $\sigma_{z|x}^2$:
   $$\sigma_{z|x}^2 = \left(\frac{1}{\sigma_z^2} + \frac{w^2}{\sigma^2}\right)^{-1}, \quad \mu_{z|x} = \sigma_{z|x}^2 \left( \frac{\mu_z}{\sigma_z^2} + \frac{w x}{\sigma^2} \right)$$
3. **Numerical Evaluation:** For an observed data point $x = 3.0$, evaluate the numerical posterior parameters $(\mu_{z|x}, \sigma_{z|x}^2)$.

*Transfer Solution:*
1. **Marginal Distribution $p(x)$:**
   - Expectation: $\mathbb{E}[x] = \mathbb{E}[w z + \epsilon] = w \mathbb{E}[z] + 0 = 2.0(0) = \mathbf{0.0}$.
   - Variance: $\text{Var}(x) = w^2 \text{Var}(z) + \sigma^2 = (2.0)^2 (1.0) + 1.0 = 4.0 + 1.0 = \mathbf{5.0}$.
   - Marginal distribution: $p(x) = \mathcal{N}(x \mid 0, 5.0)$.
2. **Posterior Formulas ($w=2, \sigma_z^2=1, \sigma^2=1$):**
   $$\sigma_{z|x}^2 = \left( \frac{1}{1.0} + \frac{2^2}{1.0} \right)^{-1} = (1 + 4)^{-1} = \frac{1}{5} = \mathbf{0.2000}$$
   $$\mu_{z|x} = 0.2000 \left( \frac{0}{1.0} + \frac{2.0 x}{1.0} \right) = 0.2000 (2 x) = \mathbf{0.4000 x}$$
   Posterior distribution: $p(z \mid x) = \mathcal{N}(z \mid 0.4 x, 0.20)$.
3. **Numerical Evaluation for $x = 3.0$:**
   - $\mu_{z|x=3} = 0.4000 \times 3.0 = \mathbf{1.2000}$.
   - $\sigma_{z|x=3}^2 = \mathbf{0.2000}$.
   *Interpretation:* Observing $x = 3.0$ shrinks our uncertainty from prior variance $1.0$ down by $5\times$ to $0.20$, shifting our most likely explanation to $z = 1.20$.

### Production Engineering Traps

| Trap | Why It Fails | Production Fix |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Attempting Monte Carlo integration on raw prior $p(z)$** | $99.999\%$ of prior samples yield $p(x \mid z) \approx 0$, producing massive estimation variance | Use **Importance Sampling** or Variational Inference with $q_\phi(z \mid x)$ |
| **Ignoring KL term in ELBO** | Latent space devolves into arbitrary disjoint points like an unregularized autoencoder | Retain KL regularization: $\beta \cdot D_{\text{KL}}(q_\phi(z \mid x) \parallel p(z))$ |
| **Setting KL weight $\beta$ too high initially** | Triggers instant posterior collapse before the decoder learns reconstruction | Apply **KL Annealing / Warmup** (gradually ramping $\beta$ from $0.0 \to 1.0$) |
| **Confusing Marginal Evidence $p(x)$ with Joint $p(x, z)$** | Optimizing joint $p(x, z)$ directly w.r.t. $z$ leads to unregularized MAP point-estimate overfitting | Always marginalize or bound: optimize the ELBO over distribution $q_\phi(z \mid x)$ |

## 13. Explain it back and return to it

**Closed-notes Feynman prompt:**  
Imagine explaining to a junior machine learning engineer why we cannot simply evaluate the probability of a picture by testing every possible hidden variable, how the Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO) provides a sturdy floor that lets us climb toward maximum likelihood, and why a model that ignores its hidden variables (posterior collapse) defeats the entire purpose of latent modeling. Do not use the terms “Leibniz rule” or “Sylvester determinant” in your initial plain-English summary. Once you finish, restore the formal terms and state the master ELBO decomposition equation.

<details>
<summary>Model explanation for self-evaluation</summary>

Imagine you see a dark shadow on a screen and want to know how likely that shadow is. You know the shadow was made by a 3D puppet, but you don't know the puppet's shape or pose. To find the exact probability of the shadow, you would theoretically have to test every possible position of every finger and joint in 3D space, calculate what shadow each makes, and add them all up. In high dimensions, there are infinitely many poses, and testing random poses almost never produces the shadow you see—making exact calculation impossible.

Instead of testing random poses blindly, we train a smart detective (the encoder network) that looks at the shadow and suggests only the few puppet poses that actually make sense. By comparing how well these suggested poses recreate the shadow against how reasonable the poses are, we calculate a score called the Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO). The ELBO is guaranteed to sit just below the true probability. By pushing the ELBO up, we force the true probability up with it.

If the puppet generator becomes lazy, it might learn to draw a generic shadow without looking at the puppet pose at all. When this happens (posterior collapse), the hidden puppet becomes completely useless, and we lose the ability to generate new specific pictures by changing the puppet.

*Restoring formal terminology:* The impossibility of testing all poses is the **intractability of continuous marginal evidence** $p(x) = \int p(x, z) dz$. The guaranteed floor is the **Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO)**, governed by the master identity $\ln p_\theta(x) = \text{ELBO}(q) + D_{\text{KL}}(q \parallel p)$, and the lazy failure is **posterior collapse** where $q_\phi(z \mid x) \to p(z)$.

</details>
